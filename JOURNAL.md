<img width="4000" height="4000" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/0d9c2b0d-9c86-406d-8c19-76499dc690de" />

The first step was making a fin can though. This is way simpler than it looks - 2mm thick tube, 1" long taper up front, 18mm fillets. I did cool variable fillets on the tips to get a nice taper. 

<img width="1141" height="1101" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8f4a31a0-2e5f-4447-bdf8-c2465d5c070a" />

My process for making these is pretty straight forward. I create copies of the fins and tube, join them together (first you must press pull the root of the fin so that the root is intersecting the tube), then fillet the root, and subtract the tube and fin body copies. Boom! Fillets that are like 99% accurate to life. The front aluminum taper is just a simple sketch and revolve. 

As for the new motor case, the main change is the solidification of the design details. 1/4" pins, 1/4-20 set screws for the lower closure, and an expansion cone. 

<img width="1929" height="303" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/334c9d3e-3644-42aa-bccf-5db64447a90b" />

<img width="1098" height="690" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/62312f38-4f0d-43d2-8a49-d662986dcb69" />

<img width="795" height="434" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/cc7d6c6e-3f6d-41a4-b39a-6d43ada2f3fe" />

The second photo showcases the nozzle architecture and expansion cone. It goes graphite throat -> phenolic carrier -> aluminum carrier -> aluminum expansion cone. The expansion cone has a lip on the outside for the fin can to sit against. Initially, the fin can was further forward and the lip was not present (well it was, but moved up) like this: 

<img width="1446" height="1003" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/bc230bcc-9173-4109-913a-4fbb42bd040f" />

However, we moved it back for two reasons. One, generally the further back your fins the more stable you are as stability is defined by the distance between your center of mass and center of pressure - moving the fins back moves the center of pressure back, away from the center of mass. Two, having more of the fin can over the nozzle section will be better for the fin can longevity. This is because that will actually be the coldest part of the case due to the sheer amount of material/thermal mass - being higher up risks melting my epoxy.

Instrumentation stack: 
The instrumentation stack is comprised of a stack of PCBs, aluminum machined parts, and nonconductive 3D printed spacers. The aluminum acts as shielding, heatsink, or robust structure mounting, while the nonconductive 3D printed parts prevent shorting of the PCBs. And of course, the PCBs do all the cool stuff! Vertically it goes: Camera module, mcu pcb, sensor module, geiger module. This is because the camera module is standalone, so it gets to be an end cap, and the geiger generates high voltage so it needs to be shielded from the other boards by the aluminum mounting. What is experiment 3? Well. I am quite excited about this one.

Air density has a big impact on resonating bodies. Lower density means less resistance which means the resonance will increase. This can actually impact the tuning of musical instruments if you go from low altitude to high altitude. 
How is this relevant? Well...
I figure I can measure air density, and thus altitude, via resonance. 
If I use a high fidelity piezo transducer and a matching high fidelity receiver I can run frequency sweeps on the airframe. By measuring the changes in the resonance response over time, I can derive air density and altitude. Pretty sweet.

# 4/18/2026 3 AM - Battery. Charging.

_Time spent: 6.75h_

This sucked. This took a while. Gah. 

So, I started off with using a DW01A and FS8205A. The DW01A is a battery protection IC that prevents both overcharge and overdrain of my battery by cutting off the negative leg of the circuit. FS8205A is simply two mosfets packaged into one chip. This is because you need one mosfet that can shut off for overdraw, and one mosfet that can shut off for overdrain. 

About two journal entries ago I inserted everything I would need - 4 battery connectors, one DW01A, and one FS8205A. 

Why 4 battery connectors? Well, I am super space constrained here. So, by running a 1s4p setup, I can get a ton more battery capacity. 4x350mah = 1400mah. Hell yeah. 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776481474820-hdfcma.png)

This is the DW01A application circuit, directly from the datasheet. Well, one of the datasheets. The official one directs to a 404 error now so I had to use one that is a little different looking but the same content. You may notice the lack of an FS8205A. Well, it's right there at the bottom! Those two weird arrow thingies coming off the D0 and C0 pins is representing two mosfets, or one FS8205A. 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776481601402-k7nk7s.png)

First the four battery connectors are wired together in parallel to create our battery bank. There is one decoupling cap, C_VBAT, to smoothen the jolt of conncting or disconnecting cells. The top rail is positive, VBAT, while the bottom is negative, GND (kinda). 
I say kinda because it needs to go through the DW01A and FS8205A before it becomes ground. 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776481742417-syf8lw.png)

So we get something like this! Initially I had a B+ and B- flag so I could have the connectors seperate from the rest of the circuitry but that was being annoying so I ditched it. OC and OD on the DW01A control overcharge and overdraw, respectively, hence them being connected to the gate pins, G2 and G1. Drain is just ground, as if the circuit begins to overdraw or overcharge, the gates will be "opened" to "drain" the power straight to ground, bypassing the battery entirely.

Except for one issue that was pointed out to me by a mechatronics friend. The way ground is set up here, being straight on the B- leg without going through the mosfets first, means that the mosfets won't actually do anything - they are directly bypassed.

Funny, because this incorrect way of wiring is directly from the datasheet ! I took this as a sign to use a slightly nicer charging IC from texas instruments. 

I spent some time looking at their offerings, as some of them have integrated mosfets, but all of those need MCU input and I really want to avoid that for simplicity and reliability's sake. 

I ended up settling on the BQ29700. This is an identically functioning chip but with an awesome datasheet. 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776482092101-hda7zj.png)

Look how much nicer this schematic is. And the ground is properly placed after the mosfets. Lovely.

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776482118935-zqn86o.png)

I actually ended up flipping the whole circuit horizontally so that it would match the example schematic provided by TI. Less chance of confusing my brain. 

You may have noticed the return of the B- flag. This was just from me misreading the datasheet circuit/trying to overcomplicate things. 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776482253219-tua1po.png)

It gets replaced with a wire. 

This leaves one last thing for the battery circuit... The charger IC itself! So far I have just been doing protection, I need something to protect the battery from. 

I chose an MCP73831T as it had a good datasheet and simple pinout. 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776482357092-iqy5jz.png)

Here is our example circuit to follow. VIN comes in the left, in my case 5v from the USBC port, and VBAT comes out the right, a carefully modulated supply to charge the battery properly. The resistor connecting the PROG and VSS pins to ground is what decides the charge rate. In my case I went with 500mA as it guarantees no impactful heat generation. 

Oh, the weird triangle thing coming off VDD and into STAT is a green status LED. Hence STAT. It blinks when charging and goes solid when fully charged (I think?). 

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1776482517099-gvejnc.png)

Here it is implemented! The lower section is the same as before with the 4 cell inputs and protection circuitry - the charger IC just got added on top with the VBAT lead going into, well, VBAT, to charge the batteries.

Not much. I must figure out the RF stuff for the mcu, finish up the USB and USB-to-serial wiring, and... actually that is it for the schematic. Then I can move on to the board itself, finally. Routing, packaging, etc.

It was at this point (it had been like two hours or something idk) I started talking to my mechatronics friend again and he questioned why I was using a mosfet for turning power on/off to the camera. I assumed it was the only way. 
It isn't! 
Turns out the boost converter I am using has an enable pin ! I can pull this pin to ground and the converter shuts off, I can pull it to.. anything higher than 0.4v, and it turns on !
So, to minimize failure risk, I am setting it up so that it defaults to being on, pulled to battery voltage. The MCU upon booting will then pull the enable pin to ground, shutting the boost off. Once recording needs to start, it will just simply release the pin back to VBAT. This also means that there will be way less RF interference until the camera turns on, so being able to achieve a stable LORA connection will be way easier. 
So I got rid of the mosfet and went back to wiring it up. Using the enable pin in this way only required two more resistors, not bad at all.

By this point I had practically finished wiring the first 3 net flags so I decided to focus in on one section - the camera power circuit.

I am pretty happy with the circuit now. To summarize it:
The circuit is a boost converter power supply setup for the camera. It is enabled/disabled through the MCU pulling the EN pin to ground (enabled by default). The power is routed to a 5 pin JST-PH connector, where two of the pins (1 and 2) and power, while 3, 4, and 5 are for interfacing with the camera module UART. 
Vbat goes in the left, 5v comes out the right, functional until 0.4v of battery voltage, long after the MCU has shut off (but the MCU shutting off does not turn the boost converter off). The battery protection circuit will trigger before that though (hopefully).

# 3/25/2026 12 AM - The Beginning

_Time spent: 1.833333333333333h_

Today was the start of the design process for my camera module. This design is mainly for a very special project that is not very public, but it is quite cool. 

I spent the hour and a half planning and prepping to design this thing. I asked a lot of questions to the lead on the project, like space constraints, needed recording time, mounting, camera, etc. 

So we have:
- Needs to be 4k 30fps footage minimum, so we will be using a Runcam Split 4 V2.
- Needs to fit inside of a 3" coupler, which has an ID of 63.5mm. 
- Needs to also fit around a second tube, maximizing how big that tube can be. Basically the module is a disc/cylinder with a fixed OD of 63.5mm and needs to have as big of an ID as possible.
- Needs to record for 45 minutes minimum.
- Needs to be bolted in from the outside of the coupler (ring of screws around the coupler)
- Needs to minimize straight section of the coupler (as this increases drag).
- Must use imperial hardware (:( )

I believe that is everything.
